\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the transfer ratio shows}
On single-hop NQ, a Hit@5 gain of a dense encoder over a small encoder came with an EM gain of \ev{T.lnq.R4} to \ev{T.lnq.R3} points per point, with both readers, and the ratio stayed well below 1 under every answer metric and baseline we tried at $k\geq 3$. The registered full-corpus run agreed for the encoders it covered: the ratio of e5-base-v2 moved by \ev{V6.dT.q} and \ev{V6.dT.l}, and both values lay inside the sub-corpus intervals. The query-level accounting shows where the difference between the two gains sits. Queries that newly retrieved a gold passage did gain EM, but \ev{V5.tr.nq.misshit.embase} of them had already been answered correctly under MiniLM-L6, and queries that kept or lost their hit gave back part of the gain. Three observations within our setup are consistent with this. (i) The readers fell well short of the gold-passage reference even with good retrieval, so part of the loss lies in reading, not retrieval. (ii) EM given a miss stayed far above closed-book EM, and the abstention prompt showed that the reader still answered most misses. The NQ relevance labels are sparse: \ev{V6.as.miss.sub.dense} of dense-encoder misses had an answer string in their top five passages, and in the blinded audit \ev{V6.au.A.yes} of 100 e5-base-v2 misses had a relevant passage with a correct answer. The readers may also have known some answers. Hit@5 therefore overstates the difference between encoders that matters for the reader. The full corpus showed the same effect from the other side: it removed about a tenth of the hits of each dense encoder but only \ev{V6.lossratio} EM points per lost hit. (iii) Queries that hit under both retrievers lost a little EM with the stronger encoder, so a change of context can cost answers even when a gold passage stays in the top five. We did not inspect these contexts; this point is a hypothesis.

On multi-hop HotpotQA, Hit@5 saturated while Both@5 did not. The EM gain sat almost entirely in queries for which the stronger encoder retrieved more supporting passages, mostly the second of two, and the EM ranking of retrievers followed Both@5 exactly. With Both@5 as the denominator, the HotpotQA ratios fell to \ev{V4.both.hp.min}--\ev{V4.both.hp.max}, close to the NQ ratios against BM25. The large Hit@5-based ratios on HotpotQA therefore largely reflect the denominator: the retrieval gain that matters is larger than Hit@5 shows. The comparison questions, where both entities are named in the question, were close to their gold-passage reference for every dense encoder. These are descriptions of the data, not causal attributions, but they point the same way: for multi-hop questions, a retrieval metric that requires all needed evidence tracks the answer far better than Hit@k.

Measured with Hit@5, the two datasets would support different conclusions about the same encoders. Measured with metrics that count the evidence the question needs, both show an answer gain that is a fraction of the retrieval gain.

\subsection{Comparison with earlier work}
That better retrieval need not give proportionally better answers is not new \citep{salemi2024erag,mathur2026policy,ghosh2026evidence}, and our NQ results agree with the falling recall conversion rate reported by \citet{peng2026neocorrag}. Our results do not contradict the strong correlations reported by \citet{samuel2026beyond} or the conclusion of \citet{hitl2026comparative} that retrieval quality is the main determinant of end-to-end quality: the EM ranking of retrievers followed their Hit@5 ranking closely in every cell (Spearman $\rho$ between \ev{RHO.lnq} and \ev{RHO.qnq}). Correlation concerns the order of systems; the transfer ratio concerns the size of the gain. A metric can rank encoders correctly and still overstate how much answers improve, and that size is what decides whether a more expensive encoder is worth its cost. What this study adds is the paired size with its uncertainty, its sensitivity to the baseline, the answer metric, $k$ and evidence completeness, and an accounting of where in the query set the gain appears.

The context experiment agrees with earlier findings on distraction. Top-ranked non-gold passages lowered EM compared with random passages \citep{cuconasu2024power,amiraz2025distracting,repantis2026competition}, and adding random passages to the gold passage changed EM by no detectable amount. Gold position showed no detectable effect among five passages, in line with \citet{cuconasu2025positional}; with five short passages our setting is far from the long contexts in which position effects were originally shown \citep{liu2024lost}. The near-identical retrieval results of exact and approximate search agree with \citet{leto2024toward}, who also measured answers; we measured retrieval only.

\subsection{Practical implications}
Within our setup, the choice of encoder mattered less for answers than for retrieval, while its retrieval-stage cost differed by more than an order of magnitude. For single-hop questions, no larger encoder gave a detectably higher EM than a base-size encoder such as e5-base-v2 or bge-base, which encoded \ev{E7.ratio.R5R4} times faster than bge-large. For multi-hop questions, bge-large gave a small but detectable gain. Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B had the highest retrieval-stage cost and gave lower EM than e5-base-v2 on HotpotQA; its instruction and length settings may not suit this task, and we did not tune them. Retrieving ten passages instead of five raised EM for most retrievers and narrowed their differences, which is another option when the reader's context allows it, although it nearly doubles the prompt the reader must process. The cross-encoder raised the Hit@5 and EM of the weak BM25 retrieval stage; for e5-base-v2 it lowered Hit@5, and no EM difference was detected. Approximate search returned nearly the same top passages as exact search at a small fraction of the latency; we did not measure its effect on answers.

\subsection{Alternative explanations}
Several features of the design could produce low NQ transfer without a property of the encoders. EM undercounts correct answers \citep{kamalloo2023}: among the 100 audited misses, the author judged \ev{V6.au.C.yes} answers correct where EM counted \ev{V6.au.EM}. The F1 and containment ratios were higher but still well below 1. The sub-corpus inflates dense retrieval scores, but on the full corpus the order of four retrievers was kept, the e5-base-v2 ratios stayed inside their sub-corpus intervals for both readers, and the ratios of bge-small and e5-base-v2 against BM25 rose only from \ev{V6.b0.sub} to \ev{V6.b0.full}. This check covers three dense encoders on NQ; the larger encoders and HotpotQA were not run on a full corpus. Answer-bearing hard negatives did not explain the context effect either: P5 held, with a larger drop, on the questions whose hard negatives contained no answer string. The readers were 4-bit quantised 7--8B models with one prompt; stronger readers may convert hits into answers at a different rate. The ratio also depends on the baseline and on $k$. Against BM25 the NQ ratios were higher (\ev{V4.b0.nq.min}--\ev{V4.b0.nq.max}), and with one passage read they were higher still. The ratio therefore describes a pair of retrievers at a given reading depth, not a single encoder.
