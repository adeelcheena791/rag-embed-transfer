\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why retrieval gains transfer only in part}
\label{sec:why-transfer}
Eq.~\ref{eq:decomp} gives a reference value for the transfer ratio.
If P(EM$\mid$hit) and P(EM$\mid$miss) stayed at the values of MiniLM-L6-v2, $T$ would equal their difference, \NUM{T.ref}.
The observed ratios at $k{=}\NUM{k.main}$, \NUM{T.lo} to \NUM{T.hi}, fall below this value.
\emph{Observation:} the point estimates of P(EM$\mid$hit) for the stronger dense encoders (\NUM{pem.hit.strong.lo} to \NUM{pem.hit.strong.hi}) lie below that of MiniLM-L6-v2 (\NUM{pem.hit.R1}), with overlapping intervals (untested).
\emph{Hypothesis (untested):} the extra hits of a stronger encoder fall on questions that the reader finds harder, so each added hit yields fewer correct answers.

Two further gaps limit transfer.
First, P(EM$\mid$hit) with five passages stays below Gold EM (\NUM{em.GOLD}), which uses only the relevant passages.
\emph{Hypothesis:} non-relevant passages in the top five distract the reader, as \citet{leto2024toward} report for added non-gold documents.
Incomplete relevance labels are an alternative explanation: a ``miss'' may contain the answer in an unjudged passage.
Second, P(EM$\mid$miss) (\NUM{pem.miss.lo} to \NUM{pem.miss.hi} for dense encoders) exceeds closed-book EM (\NUM{em.CB}).
Unjudged answer-bearing passages, partial cues and matches on common short answers could each explain this; our design cannot separate them.

BM25 shows the highest P(EM$\mid$hit) (\NUM{pem.hit.R0}).
\emph{Hypothesis:} BM25 finds the relevant passage mostly for questions with strong lexical overlap, which the reader also finds easier; this would be a selection effect rather than more useful passages.
The alternative, that the reader extracts answers more easily from lexically matched passages, cannot be ruled out with our data.

\subsection{Why the largest encoders in our set do not win}
\label{sec:why-large}
Within our setup, bge-large and Qwen3-Emb-0.6B do not retrieve better than e5-base-v2; they retrieve worse (\S\ref{sec:rq1}).
Their lack of a detectable EM gain is therefore consistent with their retrieval, not evidence of weaker transfer.
Model size alone does not determine retrieval quality in this evaluated set, consistent with \citet{baidya2026choosing}; we did not isolate training data or objective.
Our pipeline reproduces published SciFact scores for the five encoders we checked, including both of these (\S\ref{sec:models}), so a pipeline error is an unlikely cause.
Prefixes and instructions are a possible cause that we did not vary (\S\ref{sec:stages}).
Transfer also compresses differences.
With $T$ near \NUM{T.R5}, the Hit@5 gaps of e5-base-v2 over bge-large (\NUM{d.hit@5.R5.R4}\,pp) and over Qwen3-Emb-0.6B (\NUM{d.hit@5.R5.R6}\,pp) correspond to expected EM gaps below the estimated minimum detectable difference of \NUM{mdd}\,pp.
Differences in retrieval that are clearly significant can thus leave EM differences that \NUM{n.joined} queries cannot resolve.

\subsection{Practical implication}
\label{sec:implication}
\emph{Interpretation:} in our setup, a retrieval benchmark rank alone overstates the answer gain from a stronger encoder.
Within our setup and at $k{=}\NUM{k.main}$, a Hit@5 gain of $x$\,pp came with an EM gain of \NUM{T.lo}$x$ to \NUM{T.hi}$x$\,pp relative to MiniLM-L6-v2, while encoding cost rose by up to \NUM{enc.ratio.R6}$\times$ between e5-base-v2 and Qwen3-Emb-0.6B.
The ratio changes with $k$ in our ablation (\S\ref{sec:ablations}) and may depend on the reader, corpus and question type, which we did not vary; it should be measured per setup.
